# Transport operations in container terminals: Literature overview, trends, research directions and classification scheme
**Authors:** Héctor J. Carlo, Iris F.A. Vis, Kees Jan Roodbergen
**Journal:** European Journal of Operational Research (2014)

## 1. The Anatomy of an Automated Container Terminal (ACT)
To understand AGV dispatching, we must first understand the physical layout of the port. The authors divide a container terminal into 5 distinct areas:

1. **Berth:** The water area where the containership docks.
2. **Quay:** The immediate shoreline where Quay Cranes (QCs) operate to lift containers off the ships.
   - *Note: The Berth and Quay together are referred to as the **Seaside**.*
3. **Transport Area:** The physical space bridging the quay and the storage yard. This is the domain of transfer vehicles (AGVs, ALVs, Straddle Carriers). 
4. **(Storage) Yard:** The blocks where containers are temporarily stacked and stored.
5. **(Terminal) Gate:** The land-facing exit/entry point where external street trucks and trains pick up or drop off containers.
   - *Note: The Yard and Gate together are referred to as the **Landside**.*

## 2. The Transport Area as the Ultimate Bottleneck
The authors highlight that the Transport Area is the connective tissue of the entire port. Because it sits at the intersection of the seaside and landside, **any inefficiency here causes a complete system bottleneck.** 

If AGV routing is poorly optimized:
- The Seaside suffers: Quay Cranes go idle waiting for an AGV.
- The Landside suffers: Yard Cranes go idle waiting for an AGV to arrive.

This formally justifies the need for advanced, real-time dispatching algorithms (like Deep Reinforcement Learning) to minimize QC idle time and keep the transport area fluid.

## 3. The Three Decision Problems in Transport
When managing the transport area, terminal operators face three distinct levels of decision-making:
1. **Vehicle Selection:** Deciding *what* type of vehicle to buy (investment vs operational cost tradeoff).
2. **Fleet Sizing:** Deciding *how many* vehicles are required to handle the terminal's throughput.
3. **Routing and Dispatching:** Deciding *which* specific vehicle handles *which* container, and what path it takes to get there. **(This is the exact domain of our DRL agent).**

## 4. Vehicle Classification: The Synchronization Problem
The literature broadly classifies all transfer vehicles into two categories:
* **Self-lifting vehicles:** (e.g., Straddle Carriers, Automated Lifting Vehicles/ALVs). These can drive over a container on the ground and lift it autonomously. 
* **Non-lifting vehicles:** (e.g., Yard Trucks, **AGVs**). These vehicles are essentially flatbeds. They require external equipment (like a Quay Crane or Yard Crane) to physically place or remove the container from their chassis.

**The Deep Reinforcement Learning Context:** 
Because our environment uses **AGVs (Non-lifting vehicles)**, we face a massive synchronization constraint. An AGV cannot drop a box on the floor and leave; it must wait for the crane. Conversely, the Quay Crane cannot drop a box on the floor; it must wait for an AGV to arrive. This physical limitation is the fundamental reason why dispatching must be near-perfect to minimize QC idle time.

## 5. Industry Trends (Justifying AI over Math)
Section 2.2 notes that modern terminals are shifting from traditional "fixed-path" AGVs (guided by wires in the floor) to "intelligent, free-travel" AGVs guided by GPS. 
While this allows for massive flexibility, the authors note it comes at the expense of requiring *"more complicated traffic management policies."* This perfectly justifies the shift away from rigid mathematical heuristics (like the rules in Egbelu & Tanchoco, 1984) and toward dynamic, AI-driven approaches like DRL that can handle highly complex, free-roaming traffic patterns.

## 6. Traffic Management: Deadlocks and Double-Cycling
As AGVs move through the transport area, the terminal must manage traffic using **Zone Control**. To handle deadlocks, two strategies are used (often together):
1. **Deadlock Prevention:** Strictly limiting access to specific zones to mathematically prevent gridlock before it happens.
2. **Deadlock Resolution:** Allowing freer movement, but using algorithms to detect and untangle deadlocks immediately.

**Advanced Operations (Double-Cycling):** Traditionally, an AGV drops off an export container at the ship and drives back empty. Under a *double-cycling* policy, the AGV drops off an export container, and immediately picks up an import container from the same crane before driving back. This drastically reduces empty travel time but significantly increases the complexity of dispatching.

## 7. The ALV vs. AGV Efficiency Gap
In Section 5, the authors review papers comparing AGVs (non-lifting) against ALVs (self-lifting). The findings are stark:
- Vis and Harika (2004) found that a terminal requires **38% more AGVs** than ALVs to handle the exact same unloading operations.
- Yang et al. (2004) found that **50% more AGVs** are required.

**Why?** Because ALVs can drop a container on the ground and immediately drive away. AGVs must sit idle in buffer areas waiting for the Quay Crane to load/unload them. 
*Thesis Context:* This mathematically proves the necessity of advanced dispatching. Because a terminal using AGVs is inherently disadvantaged by wait times, the software/dispatching algorithm must be ultra-efficient to close the productivity gap without buying 50% more vehicles.

## 8. The Carlo et al. Classification Scheme
The authors propose a 30-attribute classification scheme to categorize all container terminal literature. This taxonomy provides the perfect vocabulary for defining new research. 

Using Carlo's exact terminology, our DRL thesis can be formally classified as follows:
* **Decision Variables:** `dispatch` (Dispatching of vehicles).
* **Vehicle Capabilities:** `non-lift` (Automated Guided Vehicles).
* **Temporal:** `readys` and `dues` (Stochastic ready/due times, aligning with our focus on stochastic disruptions).
* **Uncertainty Environment:** `stochop` (Stochastic optimization).
* **Performance Metric (Objective):** `QC` (Average quay crane work rate, i.e., minimizing QC idle time).

## 9. Routing vs. Dispatching (Defining the DRL Domain)
The authors make a strict academic distinction between two terms that are often incorrectly used interchangeably:
- **Routing (Static):** Specifying the number of trips, the paths to be taken, and the schedules in advance. Mostly solved using deterministic Integer Programming (IP) and heuristics.
- **Dispatching (Dynamic):** Determining *which* vehicle will perform *each* movement in real-time.
**Thesis Application:** Our Deep Reinforcement Learning approach falls strictly under **Dispatching**. It is a dynamic, real-time response system designed to handle stochastic disruptions, not a static routing schedule.

## 10. The Egbelu & Tanchoco Pivot (Vehicle-Initiated Dispatching)
Carlo et al. directly cite Egbelu & Tanchoco (1984) when discussing dispatching rules, but provide a massive industry-specific insight:
> *"We note that in container terminals dispatching is mainly **vehicle initiated** as there are far more requests than vehicles."*

**Thesis Application (Choosing our Architecture):** Paper 1 (Egbelu 1984) introduced both Work-Centre and Vehicle-Initiated rules, often focusing on Work-Centres (machines) calling for vehicles in a general factory. However, Carlo et al. prove that for Automated Container Terminals, the architecture must be strictly **Vehicle-Initiated**. Because the 10 AGVs will always be overwhelmed by the sheer volume of containers at the 4 QCs, the AGVs must drive the decisions. When an AGV becomes idle in the yard, **the AGV queries the DRL agent** to ask which QC it should serve next.

## 11. Clues for the DRL State Space (Inventory-Based Dispatching)
In Section 7.2, the authors review Briskorn et al. (2006), who proposed an "inventory-based" dispatching policy. This rule gives priority to the Quay Crane whose buffer has the *fewest* AGVs currently heading towards it, proving to be highly robust against stochastic travel times.
**Thesis Application:** This proves that our 114-dimensional DRL state vector **must** track the "in-transit inventory" of AGVs. The neural network needs to see exactly how many AGVs are currently en-route to QC 1 vs QC 2. Without this state information, the DRL agent might dispatch all idle AGVs to a single delayed QC, creating a massive traffic deadlock in the transport area.

## 12. Pooling vs. Dedicated Dispatching
At the end of Section 7.2, the authors conclusively state:
> *"Pooling of vehicles in dispatching outperforms dedicated vehicle dispatching."*

**Thesis Application:** The DRL agent must treat the fleet of 10 AGVs as a single, unified pool. We cannot hard-dedicate specific AGVs to specific Quay Cranes, as pooling allows for much higher utilization and dynamic response to delays.

## 13. The "Deterministic Flaw" in Integrated Models
In Section 9, the authors review models that attempt to integrate seaside, transport, and yard operations. They note a glaring flaw in the literature:
> *"On one hand, the assumption of deterministic operational times could jeopardize the practicality of the solutions. On the other hand, the assumption allows researchers to solve larger instances of the problem."*

**Thesis Application:** This provides the primary justification for using Deep Reinforcement Learning. Traditional Operations Research (OR) models must assume a "perfect," deterministic world to remain mathematically solvable. DRL does not have this limitation; it is designed specifically to handle stochastic disruptions and find optimal policies in chaotic, real-world conditions.

# 14. Final Takeaways for the Thesis
This paper provides the structural foundation for the thesis introduction and literature review.

1. **The Taxonomy:** The thesis can now be formally classified using Carlo's 30-attribute scheme: `dispatch`, `non-lift`, `readys`/`dues` (stochastic), `stochop`, and `QC` (objective).
2. **The Hardware Disadvantage:** Because the terminal uses AGVs (non-lifting) instead of ALVs (self-lifting), the vehicles suffer from severe idle times at the buffers. Literature proves AGV fleets must be 38-50% larger to match ALV throughput. **This mathematically justifies the need for advanced DRL dispatching**—we must use superior software to close the hardware efficiency gap.
3. **Vehicle-Initiated Architecture:** Because there are always more containers than AGVs, the dispatching system must be vehicle-initiated. The DRL agent will be queried by an idle AGV asking, "Where do I go next?"
4. **State Space Design:** To prevent deadlocks and mimic the highly successful "inventory-based" benchmark (Briskorn et al., 2006), the DRL state vector must include the number of AGVs currently en-route to each QC buffer.

## 15. The "Call to Action" (Future Research Avenues)
In Section 10.2, the authors outline specific avenues for future research. Avenue **RA3** is of critical importance:
> *"RA3. Developing models for estimating traffic congestion to tackle the typical deterministic travel time assumption;"*

**Thesis Application:** This is the ultimate academic justification for the thesis. The field's leading review paper formally requested models that abandon deterministic assumptions in favor of handling real, dynamic traffic congestion. The proposed Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent under stochastic disruptions is the direct answer to RA3.
