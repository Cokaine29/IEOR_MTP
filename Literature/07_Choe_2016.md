# Online Preference Learning for Adaptive Dispatching of AGVs in an Automated Container Terminal
**Authors:** Ri Choe, Jeongmin Kim, Kwang Ryel Ryu  
**Journal:** Applied Soft Computing (2016)  
**Methodology:** Online Preference Learning (OnPL) using Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) & Short Look-Ahead Simulations

## 1. The Bridge Between Classical Math and Deep RL
This paper is a vital evolutionary link in the literature. In the Introduction, the authors explicitly reference **Kim et al. (2004)** [Paper 3 in our syllabus]. They point out the fatal flaw in Kim's multi-criteria scoring function: 
Finding the optimal weight vector for the scoring function takes *hours of CPU time* using offline policy search. Therefore, when the terminal experiences unpredictable delays (weather, crane breakdowns, traffic), the static weights fail to adapt, and recalculating them in real-time is impossible.

**Thesis Application:** This formally establishes the necessity of Machine Learning. Traditional optimization is too slow to recalculate when the environment shifts.

## 2. Online Preference Learning (OnPL)
To solve the real-time adaptation problem, the authors introduce a Neural Network that relearns its preference function *after every single dispatching action*.
Instead of Q-Learning (where an agent learns values from delayed rewards), they use a **Pairwise Preference Function**:
1. When a decision is needed, the system runs a fast, short look-ahead simulation for all candidate jobs.
2. The simulation identifies the "Best Job".
3. The system generates training pairs: `(Best Job > Bad Job 1)`, `(Best Job > Bad Job 2)`.
4. These pairs are instantly fed into an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to update its weights online.

**Thesis Application:** This is an early form of Actor-Critic / supervised RL. While you will be using MAPPO (which uses PPO's clipped objective rather than pairwise preference), this paper proves that Neural Networks can handle real-time, dynamic terminal dispatching better than static formulas.

## 3. The Dual Objective (Productivity vs. CO2)
The paper identifies two conflicting objectives for AGV dispatching:
1. Maximize QC Productivity (minimize Quay Crane waiting time).
2. Minimize CO2 Emissions (which directly translates to minimizing AGV empty-travel distance).

> *"A very strong bias toward an empty-travel reduction leads to a sacrifice of services to the QCs, resulting in increased QC delays. By taking the reduction of empty travel as an explicit objective, we are prepared to trade QC productivity for a reduction of CO2 emissions."*

**Thesis Application:** This is exactly the "Starvation Trade-off" we discovered in Zheng (2022) and Angeloudis (2010)! To save AGV travel time, standard algorithms starve the distant cranes. To keep cranes fed, you *must* force AGVs to drive further. This paper explicitly defines this trade-off, providing another citation for your Reward Function design: $R = -\alpha(\text{Empty Travel}) - \beta(\text{QC Wait Time})$.

## 4. Vehicle-Initiated Dispatching
![Figure 1: Layout of an automated container terminal](/literature/choe_2016/figure1.png)

In Section 1, they distinguish between two types of dispatching:
- **Job-Initiated:** The most urgent job looks for an available vehicle.
- **Vehicle-Initiated:** An AGV that just finished its task looks for the best available job.

The authors explicitly choose **Vehicle-Initiated** dispatching.

**Thesis Application:** This is exactly how **Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MAPPO)** works! The AGVs are your autonomous agents. When an AGV becomes idle, it triggers an event, observes the terminal, and picks a job. This validates your agent perspective.

## 5. Dual Cycle Operations
A standard AGV delivery job consists of four steps:
1. Empty travel to a Handover Point (HP)
2. Reception (pickup) of the container
3. Loaded travel to the destination HP
4. Release (drop-off) of the container

The authors highlight the importance of **Dual Cycle Operation**. If an AGV drops off an export container to a Quay Crane, and that *same* Quay Crane immediately hands the AGV an import container to take back to the yard, the "Empty travel" step is completely eliminated. Efficient policies naturally learn to maximize dual cycles.

## 6. The "Failed to Converge" Problem (The Justification for PPO)
In Section 3 (Related Work), the authors drop an absolute goldmine for your thesis defense. They discuss why they are using Supervised Preference Learning instead of Reinforcement Learning (RL):
> *"We also observed that our trial of reinforcement learning of AGV dispatching policies using a more realistic and complicated state representation **failed to converge**. Achieving convergence calls for additional major research effort..."*

Why did their RL fail to converge in 2016? Because combining classic Q-learning with nonlinear Neural Networks is mathematically unstable. The weights can wildly swing out of control, destroying the policy.

**Thesis Application (The PPO Savior):** You can quote this exact line in your thesis! Choe et al. (2016) proved that standard RL diverges when applied to complex port states. This is exactly why you are using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** (invented in 2017, one year *after* this paper). PPO mathematically forces the neural network to only take small, safe update steps (using "Clipping" and "Trust Regions"), guaranteeing that the AI will converge smoothly without collapsing. MAPPO is the direct answer to Choe's convergence problem!

## 7. Generating Candidate Jobs
When an AGV becomes idle, it doesn't look at all 5,000 containers on the ship. It only looks at the **Candidate Jobs**.
A candidate job is defined as: *The first job remaining in the ongoing sequence of a QC that is not yet assigned to an AGV.* If there are 8 Quay Cranes, there are only 8 candidate jobs to choose from at any given millisecond. 

**Thesis Application:** This massively reduces the Action Space. Your MAPPO agent doesn't need to pick from thousands of containers; it only needs to pick which of the active QCs (or ASCs) it wants to service next.

## 8. Feature Engineering (The 9 Criteria)
In Section 4.1.2, they define the "State" of a candidate job. Before modern Deep RL (which can process raw grids or images), researchers had to manually hand-craft features. They define a job $x$ using a 9-dimensional vector:
- **$C_1$ (Urgency):** Time remaining until the due time.
- **$C_2$ (Competitive Advantage):** How much faster *this* AGV can arrive compared to the *next* closest AGV.
- **$C_3$ (Crane Readiness):** Time until the crane is ready to hand over the container.
- **$C_4$ (Empty Travel):** Distance to the pickup point.
- **$C_5$ (Loaded Travel):** Distance to the drop-off point.
- **$C_6$ (QC Delay):** Average delay per container at the target QC.
- **$C_7$ (Direction):** Loading vs. Discharging indicator.
- **$C_8$ (ASC Workload):** The remaining workload of the target yard block.
- **$C_9$ (Dual Cycle Likelihood):** Evaluates if handling this container will create a dual cycle (eliminating empty travel).

**Thesis Application:** When you build your Python simulation, you must decide how to represent the "Observation Space" for your MAPPO agents. You can either use a spatial grid (like Angeloudis) or a dense feature vector like this one. Notice how $C_4$ (Empty Travel) and $C_6$ (QC Delay) are explicitly coded into the state. The Neural Network *needs* to see these numbers to optimize the trade-off.

## 9. Preference vs. Absolute Scoring (The Precursor to Advantage)
In Section 4.1.3, the authors explain *why* they use Pairwise Preference instead of Kim's absolute scoring function $s(x) = \sum w_i C_i(x)$. 
They state that when running a simulation, it is almost impossible to generate a clean "Absolute Score" label to train a neural network. However, it is very easy to run a simulation and label that *Job A was better than Job B*. 

**Thesis Application (Actor-Critic Advantage):** This insight is mathematically profound for your thesis. Predicting absolute scores (Q-Values) is notoriously noisy and unstable in Deep RL. This exact realization led the AI community to invent **Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C)** and ultimately **PPO**. Instead of predicting the absolute value of an action, PPO calculates the *Advantage* (how much better Action A is compared to the baseline average). Choe's "Pairwise Preference" logic from 2016 is the conceptual precursor to the Advantage function you will use in MAPPO!

## 10. The Mathematical Objective Function (Reward Math)
In Section 4.1.4, the authors mathematically define the exact trade-off we have been discussing. The overall objective function is:
$$ \text{Objective} = w_T \cdot T_n + w_D \cdot D_n $$
Where:
- $T_n$ is the average QC processing time.
- $D_n$ is the average empty-travel distance of the AGVs.
- $w_T$ and $w_D$ are the weights assigned to each metric based on terminal priorities (Speed vs. Emissions).

**Thesis Application:** This is exactly how your MAPPO Reward Function should be structured. The agent receives a negative penalty (since we want to minimize both) equal to $- (w_T \cdot T_n + w_D \cdot D_n)$. 

## 11. Experience Replay (The Reserve Pool)
![Figure 3: Survival probability in the reserve pool](/literature/choe_2016/figure3.png)

In Section 4.2.1, they describe how the Neural Network learns. They do not just train on the most recent event and throw it away. They maintain a **Reserve Pool** of 30,000 past examples. When generating new examples, they sample randomly from this pool to update the Neural Network. They found that sampling probabilistically (allowing some very old examples to survive longer) prevented the AI from "forgetting" past lessons, unlike strict truncation (deleting the oldest memories immediately).

**Thesis Application:** This is exactly the concept of an **Experience Replay Buffer** in modern Reinforcement Learning! Your MAPPO algorithm will use a "Rollout Buffer" to store thousands of steps of terminal simulation, shuffling them into mini-batches to train the neural network stably. 

## 12. Preventing Starvation (The Min-Max Trick)
In Section 4.2.2, they reveal a genius trick for evaluating the short look-ahead simulation. When measuring the QC processing time ($T_{n+L}$), they don't just take the average. They measure the processing time of the *"most retarded QC"* (the Quay Crane that has processed the fewest containers and is falling the furthest behind).

Why? If they just used the average time, the AI would become "lazy." It would exclusively serve the closest QCs with the shortest processing times, leaving the distant QCs to completely starve. By forcing the evaluation metric to care about the *worst-performing* QC, the AI is forced to "equalize" the workload and drive to the distant cranes.

**Thesis Application (Reward Shaping):** This is a massive takeaway for your Python environment design. When you design the penalty for Crane Wait Time, you should not just take the `mean(wait_times)`. You should include a penalty based on the `max(wait_times)` across all cranes. This forces the MAPPO agent to prioritize the most starved crane!

## 13. The OnPL Algorithm & Confidence Weighting
![Figure 4: Pseudocode of OnPL algorithm](/literature/choe_2016/figure4.png)

In Section 4.3, the authors outline the pseudocode for OnPL. Interestingly, they do not treat all training examples equally. When they generate a training pair (Job A vs. Job B) from the simulation, they calculate a weight $w$ for that example (Eq 15). If the simulation shows that Job A is *massively* better than Job B, the example gets a high weight. If Job A is only *slightly* better than Job B, the example gets a low weight (because it might just be simulation noise). The Neural Network pays more attention to the high-weight examples.

## 14. Neural Network Optimizer (RPROP vs. Adam)
To update the Artificial Neural Network, the authors use **Resilient Backpropagation (RPROP)**. 
Why RPROP? In standard backpropagation, if the error gradient is very large, the weight update is very large, which can accidentally destroy the Neural Network's logic. RPROP ignores the *magnitude* of the gradient and only looks at the *sign* (positive or negative) to make safe, fixed-size updates.

**Thesis Application:** This is an amazing historical artifact to mention in your methodology! In 2016, researchers had to use RPROP to prevent neural networks from destroying themselves during online training. Today, we use the **Adam Optimizer** (which natively handles adaptive learning rates) combined with **PPO** (which mathematically clips the gradient step). Your MAPPO architecture naturally solves the exact gradient explosion problem that Choe was fighting in 2016.

## 15. Simulation Scale & Parameters

The terminal scale:
- 3,000 total containers (delivery jobs)

## 16. Injecting Stochasticity (The Pessimistic Scenarios)
![Figure 5: Distribution of containers at different blocks](/literature/choe_2016/figure5.png)

To truly test if their Neural Network could handle dynamic environments, the authors generated two datasets: $T_1$ and $T_2$.
- **$T_1$ (Easy):** Containers are placed neatly in the yard blocks directly behind their target Quay Cranes. Travel distances are short and uniform.
- **$T_2$ (Pessimistic/Hard):** They used Normal Distributions with high variance ($\sigma^2=5$) to wildly scatter containers across the port. This creates unequal workloads, traffic jams, and long travel distances. They explicitly filtered out any easy scenarios from $T_2$.

**Thesis Application:** This is exactly how you must test your MAPPO agent! Don't just test it on a perfectly organized ship. Inject stochasticity (randomized container placements, randomized crane delays) into your Python environment. A classical algorithm (like Genetic Algorithm) will collapse under $T_2$ conditions, but your MAPPO agent will adapt dynamically.

## 17. Neural Network Architecture (MLP)
In Section 5.2, they detail their Neural Network architecture. They tested both a Single-Layer Perceptron (SLP) and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). For the MLP, they empirically determined that **11 hidden nodes** was the optimal size.

**Thesis Application:** This proves that you do not need a massive, deep neural network with millions of parameters (like ChatGPT) to run a port. Because the State Space is highly compressed (just 9 criteria), a very simple, shallow MLP is completely sufficient. This is great news for your computational budget—you can train this on a laptop!

## 18. Mathematical Proof for Experience Replay
In Figure 6, they plot a Pareto frontier comparing two methods of managing their memory buffer:
- **Truncation:** Deleting the oldest memories to make room for new ones.
- **Sampling:** Randomly keeping some old memories in the pool probabilistically.

The graph clearly shows that "Sampling" dominates "Truncation" (it pushes the Pareto curve closer to the origin, meaning lower makespan AND lower empty travel). 

**Thesis Application:** This is mathematical proof that an **Experience Replay Buffer** is mandatory for Neural Network training in dynamic environments. If you just train on the most recent data (Truncation), the AI forgets how to handle rare edge cases. By randomly sampling from a Rollout Buffer, the AI retains a robust, generalized policy.

## 19. Replay Buffer Sizing and "Inertia"
Table 4 shows the performance based on the size of the reserve pool (Experience Replay Buffer). For the MLP, the sweet spot is between 60,000 and 90,000 examples. 
Interestingly, if the buffer is *too large* (e.g., 120,000), performance degrades. The authors state this is due to **"overly large inertia"**. If the buffer is full of ancient memories, the Neural Network becomes sluggish and cannot adapt quickly when the terminal environment suddenly changes.

**Thesis Application:** When you set the `buffer_size` parameter in your MAPPO algorithm, you cannot just set it to infinity. You need a buffer large enough to prevent forgetting, but small enough to allow the AI to adapt to sudden port disruptions.

Table 6 reveals a fascinating result: In the $T_1$ (Easy/Predictable) scenarios, the Neural Network actually **lost** to classical algorithms! Rolling Horizon (RH) achieved the best Makespan, and Policy Search (PS - Genetic Algorithm) achieved the best Empty Travel.

In a perfectly deterministic world, classical mathematical optimization will always find the mathematically perfect route. Neural Networks are approximators; they will always lose to exact math in a sterile vacuum.

**Thesis Application:** You must acknowledge this in your thesis! If a port operates perfectly with zero delays, zero breakdowns, and perfect stowage, a Genetic Algorithm is better than MAPPO. ## 22. The Total Failure of Q-Learning
In Table 6, standard Reinforcement Learning (Q-Learning) ranks dead last. The authors explicitly state: *"RL was dominated by all other methods."* They attribute this to its over-simplified state representation and inability to handle large variances.

**Thesis Application:** This is the ultimate justification for your thesis methodology. Basic single-agent Q-Learning (DQN) is entirely insufficient for the complexity of port routing. ## 26. The Starvation Trade-off in Action (Weight Ratios)

**Thesis Application:** This is empirical proof of the "Starvation Trade-off"! If you want your cranes to work faster, you *must* force your AGVs to drive further to fetch containers. In Section 6 (Conclusion), the authors perfectly summarize the fatal flaws of the three major competing dispatching methods, giving you the ultimate ammunition for your thesis defense:
